# Voice, perspective and register

A lawyer drafts every litigation document, but the voice changes with the document. Get the speaker wrong and the document fails at its job: an affidavit that argues like counsel reads as coached and invites cross-examination; a pleading that argues is liable to be struck out as embarrassing; a submission that testifies has no evidence behind it. Before writing a word, settle who is speaking, to whom, and on what basis.

## Who is speaking

| Family | Drafted by | Whose voice | Person | Addressed to | May contain | Never contains |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Pleadings** (claim, defence, reply, counterclaim) | Counsel or solicitors | The party's case, as the party's allegations. Where the court requires verification, the party adopts every allegation as true | Third person: "The [Plaintiff] avers", "The [Defendant] denies". Never "I" or "we" | The court and the other side. A pleading defines the issues | Material facts in summary form, particulars where the rules demand them, the relief claimed | Evidence, law, argument, case citations, rhetorical questions, adjectives about the other side's conduct |
| **Evidence** (affirmation, affidavit, witness statement, declaration) | Counsel or solicitors, from the witness's instructions | The witness's own account | First person singular: "I". Others by role | The court, as evidence the witness may be cross-examined on | What the witness did, saw, heard or knows; information and belief, with its source; documents exhibited where the fact is stated | Submissions, legal tests, case citations, counsel's signposts. Law appears only as "I am advised and verily believe that …" (or the local equivalent) and is left to counsel |
| **Submissions** (skeleton, brief, reply, hearing note, opening, closing, appeal, costs) | Counsel | Counsel as advocate | Impersonal: "It is submitted that", "[P]'s case is". No "I" in a written paper; a private speaking note may use it | The court ("the Court", "this Court") | The application, the law and authorities, the analysis, the orders sought | A fact without an evidence reference; counsel's personal opinion; new evidence |
| **Advice** | Counsel | Counsel's own opinion | First person singular: "I am asked", "I am of the view" | Instructing solicitors or the client's lawyers | Issues, principles, application, a hedged view, the information still needed | Numeric odds, imperatives aimed at the client, praise of the client's case |
| **Correspondence** | Solicitors | The firm, for its client | First person plural: "We refer to …", "our client" | The other side's lawyers (and, later, the court on costs) | Requests, demands, proposals, reservations of rights | Argument beyond what the letter needs; anything the firm would not want read by the judge, unless the letter is marked without prejudice |
| **Procedural papers** (summons, notice of motion, draft order) | Solicitors or counsel | The court's or the applicant's formal voice | Formula | The court and all parties | The relief sought and the procedural details | Argument and evidence |

### Holding the evidence voice

The deponent is a person, not a mouthpiece. Before drafting evidence, find out who they are: their role, their capacity (a party in person, a director authorised by a company, a non-party), what they know first-hand and what they were told, and whether they read English. Then write in words that person would use and could defend under cross-examination.

- A director deposing for a company speaks for the company's records and their own dealings. A family-law party may express feeling in moderation. A non-party says what they did and what they hold, and nothing about the merits.
- Every statement of information and belief names its source ("I am informed by [name] and verily believe that …"; "My belief is based on …"), and says plainly what the deponent does not know.
- A legal consequence is law, even when it sounds like a fact: that time has run out, that a right will be lost, that an order binds someone, that a document is privileged. Put it as "I am advised and verily believe that …" or leave it to counsel. "If time is not extended I will lose my appeal" in the witness's own voice is a submission.
- Argument may come in only through the arrangement of the facts: dates placed side by side, the other side's own words quoted back, a document exhibited at the point it proves something. Never through "It is submitted", a legal test, or a citation.
- A witness statement for trial uses its own grammar: no "I am advised"; second-hand matter is "my understanding"; any evaluation is kept to a short closing section.

### Holding the pleading voice

Plead facts, in the party's name, in summary form. Strong words belong only in the admissions, denials and the facts pleaded. The one short sentence a pleading may use for effect is a factual one ("No reply was received."). Never argue the case, cite a case, or describe the other side's conduct with adjectives. The standard for what facts must appear differs by jurisdiction; read the profile.

### Holding the advocate's voice

Every fact in a submission points back to the evidence or the record with a bundle reference (or `[Bundle ref]`). The advocate never vouches for a fact personally. Law comes from authorities the user supplied (see `authorities.md`).

## The voice

These skills write in a tight, direct register:

- **Short sentences, varied.** Aim for about 20 words a sentence on average, with many under ten. A long paragraph often ends in a short verdict sentence: "That failed." "It is not." "No reply was received."
- **Everything numbered.** Paragraphs run 1, 2, 3 continuously; sub-points are (a), (b), then (i), (ii). No bullet points in a court document.
- **Defined terms inline.** Define a term in brackets at first use ("the Agreement"), then use it consistently. Announce party abbreviations up front.
- **Concede into an alternative.** "Even if [D]'s case is taken at its highest (which is denied), …"; "Save that …, paragraph 4 is admitted."
- **No recap.** A submission ends on one sentence that characterises the opponent's position, then the orders sought. Evidence ends on the prayer. Advice ends on a short formal close. Nothing restates the argument.
- **The order drafted in full.** When a document asks for an order, write the order as the court would make it: numbered paragraphs, deadlines as dates, and the costs terms.

### Punctuation

No dash punctuation of any kind: no em dash, no en dash, and no hyphen with spaces round it doing a dash's job. Use a comma, brackets, a colon before a list, or a new sentence. Hyphens inside compound words ("strike-out", "pre-action") and ranges between numbers ("§§9-13", "pages 12-15") are not dashes and stay.

### Tells to keep out

These patterns mark text as machine-written, and judges notice them. Do not use them:

- balanced groups of three in running prose ("unexplained, unparticularised and prejudicial") where the facts give two or four;
- chains of antitheses ("This is not X; it is Y.") and "not just X but Y";
- stacked metaphors and set-piece imagery;
- a rhetorical question used to open a document or a section;
- "underscore(s)", "what can only be described as", "it is important to note", "it is worth noting", "delve", "in conclusion", "in summary", "to summarise";
- a closing paragraph that restates the thesis;
- abstract bold labels that open paragraphs ("**Nature of the rule:**"). Labels keyed to the other side's paragraphs ("[D§12-14]:") are fine;
- question-and-answer catechisms with "Answer:";
- sentence fragments used as stylish closers;
- a comma inside a closing quotation mark when the quotation does not include it.

### Trade register is not a tell

Litigation has fixed formulae, and they stay word for word: the traverse language of a defence, the opening and closing words of an affidavit, "It is submitted that", "In the premises", "taken at its highest (which is denied)", "save and except", "further and/or alternatively", "is put to strict proof", "to be taxed if not agreed", "with a certificate for counsel". So do the near-synonym pairs and triplets of pleading register ("right, title and interest"). Each skill keeps its family's formulae in `references/formulae.md`. Do not "simplify" them, and do not let a humanizer pass rewrite them.

### Human slips

When the user gives you their own draft to edit, keep their settled phrasing and constructions, even ones a style guide would change; they are the author's voice. Do not add errors to make text look human. Do catch the mistakes that change meaning or look careless to a judge: a wrong party, a stale cross-reference, a sum that differs between two paragraphs, a heading typo, an unamended prayer after an amendment.

## The register pass

These skills carry their own register pass; they need no other skill. As the last step before the checker, reread the running prose against this file: cut the tells listed above, vary sentence length, end paragraphs on the point rather than on a summary, and replace any dash. Leave untouched what must survive verbatim: every formula in `references/formulae.md`, every citation and pinpoint, every bundle reference, defined term, sum and date, and every placeholder. Then run `scripts/litcheck.py`, which tests the dash rule and the tells mechanically.
